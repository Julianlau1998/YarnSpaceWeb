How to increase or decrease evenly

“Increase 8 stitches evenly across the row” is one of the most common lines in a pattern, and one of the most annoying to work out on the spot. Here's the method, with examples you can follow.

Increasing evenly across a row

  1. Divide the stitches on the needle by the number of increases.
  2. If it divides evenly, knit that many stitches, then increase, and repeat.
  3. If there's a remainder, some sections get one stitch more: as many as the remainder.

Example: 100 stitches, increase 8. 100 ÷ 8 = 12 remainder 4, so 4 sections of 13 stitches and 4 of 12 (4 × 13 + 4 × 12 = 100). Work *knit 13, make 1* four times, then *knit 12, make 1* four times; mixing the long and short sections spreads them even better. You end with 108 stitches.

Knitting flat? Start and end with half a section, so there's no increase right at the edge: knit 6 or 7, then the repeats, and the leftover stitches at the end.

Decreasing evenly across a row

A decrease such as knit two together uses two stitches, so count it inside the section.

Example: 100 stitches, decrease 8. Sections of 12 or 13 stitches again (4 of each). Each one ends with the two stitches you knit together: *knit 11, knit 2 together* four times and *knit 10, knit 2 together* four times. You end with 92 stitches.

Over many rows, for sleeves and waists

Here the question is how often to work an increase row. For a sleeve that increases at both edges, every increase row adds 2 stitches.

Example: add 14 stitches over 60 rows. That's 7 increase rows. 60 ÷ 7 = 8 remainder 4, so 4 increases every 9th row and 3 every 8th row (4 × 9 + 3 × 8 = 60). Start with the longer gaps at the cuff and the shorter ones toward the top, or alternate them.

Crochet works the same

Increasing is working two stitches into one; decreasing is working two together. The arithmetic, sections of the stitch count divided by the number of changes, is exactly the same.
