US vs. UK crochet terms: conversion chart

American and British crochet patterns use the same stitch names for different stitches. A “double crochet” in a UK pattern is a much shorter stitch than a “double crochet” in a US pattern, and if you mix them up, your project comes out much taller or shorter than it should. This chart shows which stitch is which, and the rest of the guide helps you work out which system a pattern is written in.

Conversion chart

The stitches themselves are identical on both sides of the Atlantic. Only the names are shifted: in most cases, the UK name is one step “taller” than the US name for the same stitch.

Chain and slip stitch have the same name in both systems, so they're the only stitches you can't get wrong. Decreases and post stitches follow the stitch they're built on: if the base stitch shifts by one name, so does the decrease or post stitch.

How to tell which terms a pattern uses

Good patterns say it up front, often right under the materials list: “written in US terms” or “UK terminology”. If yours doesn't, these clues help:

  • Single crochet means US. The UK system has no “single crochet” at all. If you see sc anywhere, the pattern is American.
  • Half treble means UK. The US equivalent is half double crochet, so htr points to British terms.
  • Watch the shortest stitch. Amigurumi and dense fabrics are worked mostly in the shortest stitch after slip stitch. In a US pattern that's sc, in a UK pattern dc. An amigurumi worked in “dc” is almost always UK.
  • Look at the vocabulary. Gauge, skip and yarn over suggest US; tension, miss and yarn round hook suggest UK. British spellings like “colour” are a hint too, though not proof.
  • Check the hook size and yarn. Hooks given only in millimeters and yarn called DK or aran lean UK; letter hooks and worsted lean US.
  • Check where it's from. Patterns from the UK, Ireland, Australia and New Zealand traditionally use UK terms; patterns from the US and Canada use US terms. Online designers sometimes pick either, so treat this as the weakest clue.

If a pattern only ever mentions ch, dc and tr, it could be either. Then the photo helps: compare the stitch height in the picture with what you'd get from each reading, or crochet a small swatch and compare it with the stated gauge. A US double crochet is noticeably taller than a UK double crochet, so the row count gives it away quickly.

Common pitfalls

  • Translating only some stitches. When you convert a pattern, change every stitch name at once. Mixing systems within one pattern is the most common source of wonky rows.
  • Assuming “dc” is always the same. It's the stitch most often mixed up. Before you start, write down what dc means in this particular pattern.
  • Forgetting the turning chain. Turning chain lengths follow the stitch, not the name. A row of US dc (UK tr) usually starts with 3 chains, a row of US sc (UK dc) with 1.
  • Trusting a pattern that converted itself. Some patterns are published in both systems, and errors can sneak in during the conversion. If something looks off, compare it with the other version or the chart.
  • Ignoring gauge. Even with the right stitch, your tension can differ from the designer's. Matching gauge matters more than matching the hook size printed in the pattern.

A quick example

A US pattern says: “Ch 20, sc in 2nd ch from hook and in each ch across, turn. Ch 3, dc in each st across.” In UK terms the same instructions read: “Ch 20, dc in 2nd ch from hook and in each ch across, turn. Ch 3, tr in each st across.” Both describe exactly the same piece; only the labels changed.

Frequently asked questions

Is a UK double crochet the same as a US double crochet?

No. A UK double crochet (dc) is the stitch Americans call single crochet (sc). A US double crochet is called a treble (tr) in the UK.

How do I know if a pattern uses US or UK terms?

Look for stitch names that only exist in one system: single crochet (sc) means US terms, half treble (htr) means UK terms. Words like gauge and skip point to the US, tension and miss to the UK. Many patterns also say which terms they use near the top.

Does Australia use US or UK crochet terms?

Australian and New Zealand patterns traditionally use UK terms, although some designers write in US terms for an international audience. Always check the stitch list at the start of the pattern.

Are crochet charts different in the US and UK?

No. Crochet chart symbols are largely international: a cross or plus sign is the stitch the US calls single crochet and the UK calls double crochet, and a tall T with one slash is the stitch the US calls double crochet and the UK calls treble. A chart reads the same everywhere.
