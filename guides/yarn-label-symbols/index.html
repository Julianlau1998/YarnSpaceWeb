How to read a yarn label: symbols and care

The small paper band around a ball of yarn, the ball band, packs a lot into very little space: what the yarn is made of, how far it goes, which needles suit it, how it should knit up and how to wash it. Most of it is written in symbols and numbers. Here's what each part means and which details are worth keeping once the band comes off.

Brand, yarn name and color

The biggest print is usually the brand and the yarn's name, for example “Merino Soft”. Below it you'll find the color, often as both a name and a number, such as “Rust, color 0412”. The number is what counts: color names are sometimes translated or reused, while the number identifies the shade within that yarn line. When you buy more yarn or look for the same color in a shop, give the color number.

Dye lot: the number that matters most

Next to the color number there's a second number, labeled dye lot, lot or batch. It identifies the dye bath the yarn came from. Dyeing is never perfectly repeatable, so two dye lots of the same color can differ slightly in tone. In the ball you won't notice; in a finished sweater, a new lot can show as a clear stripe across the body.

  • Buy all the yarn for a project from the same dye lot, and a ball extra if you're unsure. Work out the amount first with the yarn amount guide.
  • Keep one band per project or note the lot number, so you can look for matching yarn later.
  • If you have to mix lots, alternate between the old and new ball every two rows for a while, so the change blends in instead of forming a line. Edges, ribbing and sleeves are good places to use up a different lot.

Yarns labeled “no dye lot” are dyed to a standard that makes lots interchangeable. Hand-dyed yarns, on the other hand, can vary even within one lot.

Fiber content

The fiber content is given in percent, for example “75 % wool, 25 % polyamide”. It tells you how the yarn behaves: wool is warm and elastic, cotton is cool and less stretchy, polyamide (nylon) adds durability for socks, and alpaca and mohair add halo and warmth. In the EU, fiber content has to be listed on textile products. Look out for superwash: that wool has been treated so it can go in the washing machine without felting. Untreated wool can felt with heat and agitation. When you substitute a yarn, fiber is one of the first things to compare.

Weight and length

Every label gives the weight of the ball and its length, for example “50 g, 125 m (137 yd)”. Together they tell you how thick the yarn is: the more meters per gram, the thinner the yarn. 125 m per 50 g is a typical DK to light worsted weight; 400 m per 100 g is a fingering or sock yarn. Because balls come in different sizes, compare meters per 100 g rather than meters per ball.

Some American labels also show a numbered skein symbol from 0 (lace) to 7 (jumbo). That's the Craft Yarn Council's yarn weight category, a quick way to group yarns of similar thickness.

Needle size and gauge square

A small drawing of two crossed knitting needles shows the recommended needle size, for example “3.5–4 mm” or “US 4–6”. A crochet hook drawing, if there is one, gives the hook size. If your pattern uses another system, look the size up in the needle and hook size chart.

The square grid next to it is the gauge square. It shows how many stitches (across) and rows (up) fit into a 10 × 10 cm or 4 × 4 in square, for example “22 sts × 30 rows”, usually in stockinette with the recommended needles. That's the manufacturer's average, not a promise: your own hands can be tighter or looser. Always knit a gauge swatch before you start a fitted project.

Care symbols explained

The care symbols follow the international ISO 3758 system (in Europe often known as GINETEX symbols). There are five basic shapes, always in the same order: washtub for washing, triangle for bleaching, square for drying, iron for ironing and circle for professional cleaning. An X through any symbol means “don't”. Bars underneath mean a gentler treatment: one bar is mild, two bars very mild.

American labels sometimes use dots inside the washtub instead of a number: the more dots, the warmer the water. For wool, the combination you'll see most often is hand wash or wool cycle at 30 °C, no bleach, no tumble dryer, dry flat and iron at low to medium temperature. Drying knits flat keeps them from stretching under their own weight.

Frequently asked questions

What is the difference between color number and dye lot?

The color number names the shade, and it stays the same for years. The dye lot identifies the batch the yarn was dyed in. Two balls with the same color number but different dye lots can differ slightly in tone, which often shows as a visible stripe in the finished piece.

What does the square with numbers on a yarn label mean?

That's the gauge square. It shows how many stitches and rows fit into a 10 × 10 cm (4 × 4 in) square, usually in stockinette with the recommended needle size, for example 22 stitches and 30 rows.

What does a hand in a washtub mean?

Hand wash only, at a maximum of 40 °C (104 °F). Many wool yarns that are not superwash carry this symbol, because machine washing can make them felt.

Do the care symbols apply to my finished project?

They describe how the yarn can be treated. If you combine yarns, follow the most delicate one. Washing your gauge swatch first is the safest way to see how the fabric reacts.
