How many stitches for a sweater

Whether you're designing your own sweater or adapting a pattern to your gauge, the stitch counts all come from a few measurements: your chest, how much ease you want, your upper arm and your wrist. Multiply by your gauge, round sensibly, and the sweater fits. Here's the whole process, with one full example from measuring tape to raglan.

Step 1: Measure and choose the ease

Measure around the fullest part of your chest, with the tape snug but not tight. Better still, measure a sweater you love wearing: lay it flat, measure across just below the armholes and double it. That gives you the finished size directly.

Ease is the difference between your body and the finished garment. It's what makes the same chest measurement a fitted sweater or a slouchy one:

FitEase at the chestFinished size for a 91 cm (36 in) chest
Very close−5 to −10 cm (−2 to −4 in)81–86 cm (32–34 in)
Close091 cm (36 in)
Classic+5–10 cm (2–4 in)96–101 cm (38–40 in)
Loose+10–15 cm (4–6 in)101–106 cm (40–42 in)
Oversized+15 cm (6 in) or more106 cm (42 in) or more

These ranges follow the Craft Yarn Council's fit guidelines. Thick yarn needs a little more ease than fine yarn to feel equally roomy, because the fabric itself takes up space on the inside.

Step 2: Knit a swatch for your gauge

You need both stitch and row gauge, measured on a washed and dried gauge swatch in the stitch you'll use for the body. On a sweater, a small gauge error multiplies quickly: in DK weight, being off by half a stitch per 10 cm changes a 100 cm (40 in) chest by more than 2 cm (about 1 in), and a whole stitch by almost 5 cm (2 in).

Step 3: Stitches for the body

Body stitches = finished chest circumference × stitches per cm (or per inch). Then:

  • In the round: that's your total. Front and back each get half.
  • Knit flat in pieces: halve it for front and back, and add one edge stitch at each side of each piece for the seams.
  • Round to your rib: for 2×2 rib in the round, the total should be a multiple of 4; for 1×1 rib, a multiple of 2.

Step 4: Sleeves

Measure around the upper arm and the wrist. For a classic fit, add about 5 cm (2 in) at the upper arm and 4–5 cm (1½–2 in) at the cuff, and multiply by your gauge. The difference between the two counts is the number of stitches you increase along the sleeve, two per increase round. Your row gauge tells you how many rounds the sleeve has, and therefore how often to increase. The guide to increasing evenly covers spreading them over many rows.

A useful sanity check comes from Elizabeth Zimmermann's percentage system: the upper arm is usually around a third of the body stitches, the cuff about a fifth, and the neck around 40 %.

A full worked example

Chest 36 in (91 cm), classic fit. DK yarn with 22 stitches and 30 rows per 10 cm (4 in), knit in the round from the bottom up.

  1. Finished chest: 36 + 4 in ease = 40 in (about 100 cm).
  2. Body: 40 × 5.5 stitches per inch = 220 stitches. In metric: 100 × 2.2 = 220. 220 is a multiple of 4, so it works for 2×2 rib. Front 110, back 110. Knitting in pieces instead? 110 + 2 edge stitches = 112 for each piece.
  3. Upper arm: 12 in (30 cm) + 2 in (5 cm) ease = 14 in (35 cm). 14 × 5.5 = 77, rounded to an even 76 so the sleeve increases work out in pairs (35 × 2.2 = 77 in metric too). That's 76 ÷ 220 ≈ 35 % of the body.
  4. Cuff: wrist 6¼ in (16 cm) + about 1¾ in (4 cm) = 8 in (20 cm). 8 × 5.5 = 44 stitches (20 × 2.2 = 44), exactly 20 % of the body.
  5. Sleeve increases: 76 − 44 = 32 stitches, so 16 increase rounds of 2 stitches each. The sleeve is 16 in (40 cm) from the top of the cuff to the underarm; at 30 rows per 10 cm that's 40 × 3 = 120 rounds. Increase every 7th round 16 times (112 rounds), then knit the last 8 rounds without increasing: 112 + 8 = 120.

Raglan basics

A raglan worked from the neck down grows by 8 stitches every increase round: one at each side of four raglan lines, so front, back and both sleeves gain 2 stitches each. At the underarm the sleeves go on hold, and you cast on a few stitches under each arm to join the body.

Continuing the example, with 10 stitches cast on under each arm:

  • Body at the underarm: 220 stitches, so front and back are 100 each before the 2 × 10 underarm stitches (100 + 100 + 20 = 220).
  • Sleeves at the underarm: 76 stitches, so each sleeve has 66 before the 10 underarm stitches.
  • Yoke at its widest: 100 + 100 + 66 + 66 = 332 stitches.
  • Neck: cast on 92 stitches (front 40, sleeve 6, back 40, sleeve 6), which is about 42 cm (16¾ in) around.
  • Increases: 332 − 92 = 240 stitches, ÷ 8 = 30 increase rounds. Worked every other round, that's 60 rounds, or 20 cm (8 in) of raglan depth.

This is simplified: real patterns usually raise the back neck with short rows and often make the raglan increases at different rates for body and sleeves, but the arithmetic stays the same.

Round yoke basics

A round yoke has to get from the same 92 neck stitches to the same 332 stitches, but instead of four increase lines it uses a few increase rounds spread over the yoke depth, for example four rounds of 60 increases each (92 → 152 → 212 → 272 → 332). The first rounds have many increases relative to the stitches on the needle, so space them out evenly around.

Frequently asked questions

How much ease should a sweater have?

For a classic fit, add 5–10 cm (2–4 in) to your chest measurement. A close fit has no ease, a loose fit 10–15 cm (4–6 in), and an oversized sweater 15 cm (6 in) or more.

How many stitches do I need for an adult sweater?

Multiply the finished chest circumference by your gauge. A 100 cm (40 in) sweater takes 220 stitches around at 22 stitches per 10 cm (4 in) in DK, or 180 stitches at 18 stitches per 10 cm in worsted weight.

Can I knit a pattern size at a different gauge?

Yes, if you recalculate. Divide the pattern's stitch count by your stitches per centimeter or inch to see the finished width you'd get, then pick the size whose numbers come out closest to the measurements you want.
